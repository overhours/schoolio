MODULE 01 · FOUNDATIONS

Wave fundamentals

Get the definitions right — they appear throughout the topic.

01 / 07
〰

What is a wave?

A wave is an oscillation or vibration that transfers energy from one place to another without transferring matter overall.

Exam phrase: waves transfer energy, not matter.
↕

Transverse waves

Oscillations are perpendicular (at 90°) to the direction of energy transfer.

  • Electromagnetic waves
  • Waves on a string
  • Water surface waves (simplified model)
↔

Longitudinal waves

Oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer.

  • Sound waves in air
  • Compressions: particles close together
  • Rarefactions: particles spread out

Wave anatomy

Learn these four terms so you can label a wave diagram.

Amplitude is measured from the rest position to a crest or trough.

The four key quantities

  • Amplitude: maximum displacement from the rest position. Bigger amplitude means more energy transferred.
  • Wavelength (λ): distance between matching points on consecutive waves, in metres.
  • Frequency (f): number of complete waves passing a point per second, in hertz (Hz).
  • Period (T): time taken for one complete wave, in seconds (s).
Don't mix up frequency and period: a higher frequency means a shorter period.

MODULE 03 · CALCULATIONS

Equations & calculator

Learn the equations, then practise substituting values and rearranging.

03 / 07

Wave speed equation

v = f × λ
v · speed (m/s)f · frequency (Hz)λ · wavelength (m)

Rearrange: f = v ÷ λ and λ = v ÷ f.

Example: f = 5 Hz, λ = 2 m. So v = 5 × 2 = 10 m/s.

Frequency and period

T = 1 ÷ f

Frequency and period are reciprocals. If frequency = 20 Hz, then T = 1 ÷ 20 = 0.05 s.

T · period (s)f · frequency (Hz)
Remember: 1 Hz means one complete wave every second.

MODULE 04 · ELECTROMAGNETIC WAVES

The EM spectrum

Seven waves, one family. Select a wave to explore its properties.

04 / 07

All electromagnetic waves are transverse, transfer energy and can travel through a vacuum. In a vacuum, they all travel at 3 × 10⁸ m/s.

← Long wavelength · low frequencyShort wavelength · high frequency →
Selected wave

Radio waves

Radio waves have the longest wavelengths and lowest frequencies in the EM spectrum.

Uses & risks

Used for radio and television broadcasting and communications. Non-ionising; can cause heating at high intensities.

Frequency, wavelength & energy

In a vacuum, wave speed is constant. As frequency increases, wavelength decreases. Higher-frequency EM waves carry more energy per photon.

c = f × λ

c is the speed of light in a vacuum: 300,000,000 m/s.

Ionising radiation

Ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays can be ionising. They can remove electrons from atoms, damaging cells and DNA and increasing cancer risk.

Protection depends on the radiation: reduce exposure time, increase distance and use suitable shielding. Dense materials such as lead can help shield X-rays and gamma rays.
MODULE 05 · WAVE BEHAVIOUR

Reflection, refraction & diffraction

Understand what happens when waves meet boundaries, gaps and new materials.

05 / 07
↩

Reflection

A wave bounces off a surface. The angle of incidence equals the angle of reflection.

Angles are measured from the normal, not the surface.
⤴

Refraction

A wave changes direction because its speed changes when it enters a different medium.

  • Frequency stays the same.
  • Wavelength changes.
  • Slows down: bends towards the normal.
  • Speeds up: bends away from the normal.
◎

Diffraction

Waves spread out as they pass through a gap or around an obstacle.

  • Most noticeable when gap size is similar to wavelength.
  • Longer wavelengths spread out more through the same gap.
  • Explains why sound can be heard around corners.

Refraction: the key exam idea

When a wave enters a new medium at an angle, its speed changes. This causes the wave to change direction. The frequency does not change at the boundary, so its wavelength must change too.

Use this chain in explanations: medium changes → speed changes → wavelength changes → direction may change.
MODULE 06 · SOUND

Sound & ultrasound

Sound is a longitudinal wave that travels through vibrating particles.

06 / 07

How sound travels

Vibrations make particles oscillate back and forth, creating compressions and rarefactions. The disturbance transfers energy through the medium.

  • Sound needs a medium: solid, liquid or gas.
  • Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.
  • Higher frequency = higher pitch.
  • Greater amplitude = louder sound.

Human hearing

The typical human hearing range is approximately 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz, although it varies between people and with age.

20 Hz — 20 kHz

Below this range is infrasound. Above it is ultrasound.

Ultrasound

Ultrasound has a frequency greater than 20,000 Hz.

  • Medical imaging: echoes build an image.
  • Industrial testing: detects internal cracks.
  • Sonar: measures underwater distances.

Echo distance calculation

distance = (speed × time) ÷ 2

The sound pulse travels to the object and back. Divide by two to calculate the one-way distance.

Example: echo time = 0.4 s, speed = 1500 m/s. Distance = (1500 × 0.4) ÷ 2 = 300 m.

Why use ultrasound?

Ultrasound can be reflected at boundaries between different tissues. The time taken for echoes to return helps calculate distances and build images.

It is useful for imaging soft tissues and monitoring pregnancy without using ionising radiation.

MODULE 07 · PRACTICAL SKILLS

Investigating waves

Be ready to describe a method, identify variables and improve reliability.

07 / 07

Measuring wave speed in a ripple tank

  1. Set up a ripple tank with a shallow layer of water and a vibrating dipper.
  2. Use a lamp and screen to make the wave pattern easier to see.
  3. Measure the distance across several wavefronts with a ruler.
  4. Divide by the number of wavelengths to find one wavelength.
  5. Measure the frequency of the dipper (or use its known setting).
  6. Calculate wave speed using v = f × λ.

Variables & improvements

  • Independent variable: frequency of the dipper (if investigating how frequency affects wavelength).
  • Dependent variable: wavelength or calculated wave speed.
  • Control variables: water depth and tank conditions.
  • Measure across several wavelengths to reduce percentage uncertainty.
  • Repeat measurements and calculate a mean.
  • Keep the water depth constant.

Examiner-style practical question

A student measures 10 wavefront gaps across a distance of 12 cm. The dipper frequency is 8 Hz. Calculate the wave speed.

Wavelength = 12 cm ÷ 10 = 1.2 cm = 0.012 m. Wave speed = f × λ = 8 × 0.012 = 0.096 m/s. Remember to convert centimetres into metres.

EXAM TECHNIQUE

Exam practice

Short-answer questions with mark-scheme points to check your wording.

PRACTISE

1. State the difference between a transverse and a longitudinal wave. [2 marks]

Write your answer in your head, then reveal the marking points.

1 mark: transverse oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. 1 mark: longitudinal oscillations are parallel to the direction of energy transfer.

2. Explain why a wave changes direction when it is refracted. [3 marks]

Think about what happens to speed and wavelength.

The wave enters a different medium; its speed changes; this causes its wavelength to change while frequency stays the same, so the wave changes direction if it enters at an angle.

3. Describe two dangers of exposure to ultraviolet radiation. [2 marks]

Be specific about the effect on the body.

Any two: skin damage / sunburn; eye damage; damage to DNA; increased risk of skin cancer. Award one mark for each valid point.

4. A wave travels at 24 m/s and has a wavelength of 3 m. Calculate its frequency. [3 marks]

Equation, substitution and final answer with unit.

f = v ÷ λ; f = 24 ÷ 3; f = 8 Hz.
